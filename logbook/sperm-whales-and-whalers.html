Logbook · entry 04 · a second look at a famous result

One ground, four voyages

In 2021 three researchers showed that American whalers got much worse at striking sperm whales within a few years of reaching each new North Pacific region, and asked whether the whales had learned from one another how to get away. They published every file needed to check it, so we checked. The result reproduces, and it holds when we shake it voyage by voyage. Then we asked where the evidence lives. Most of it sits in the regions whose first sighting in the 2021 data falls in 1822, mainly the Japan grounds, and in the logs of four voyages. Guide rating: sturdy on one ground, unproven on the rest

Hidden Tide · the logbook, archive edition · 2026

The one-paragraph version. Whitehead, Smith and Rendell (2021, Biology Letters) found that the share of sighted sperm whales that whalers struck fell by about 58% within a few years of whaling reaching a North Pacific region, and asked whether rapid social learning explains it. We re-ran their published data: 58.19%, time scale 2.45 years. Resampling whole voyages 10,000 times puts the drop's 95% interval at 41.9% to 69.3%. But regions whose first sighting in the 2021 data falls in 1822, mainly the Japan grounds, hold 138 of the 190 sighting-days from the crucial first three years. Counted by voyage instead of by region, four voyages supply 148 of those 190 early days. Without the 1822 regions, a plain before-and-after comparison gives a 31% drop (53% on all the data), with a 95% interval from -37% to 56% that includes zero. The famous result stands where the data are dense. Testing its "large scale" part needs more early logbooks: the 502 sealed ones we found would add about 61 early sighting-days, against 150 needed.

00A famous drop

A nineteenth-century whaling logbook is a diary of encounters: each day, where the ship was, whether whales were seen, whether the boats got one fast. Maury abstracted many such logs for his 1851 whale chart, and the Census of Marine Life digitised more. Together they cover roughly one American whaling voyage in ten.

Hal Whitehead, Tim Smith and Luke Rendell used those days to ask a sharp question. For each day sperm whales were sighted in the North Pacific between 10 and 50 degrees north, they looked back for the first sighting within 1,000 km: the moment whaling, as far as the logs know, reached that patch of ocean. Then they counted whales struck per day of sightings, year by year. In their words, the share of sighted whales that whalers "succeeded in harpooning (striking)... fell by about 58% over the first few years", with a fitted time scale of about 2.4 years.

Their title ends in a question mark, and the paper earns it. The authors weighed rival explanations: better first whalers, the most vulnerable whales dying first, and each family unit learning from its own close calls. Of the causal models they fitted, the best was one in which wary groups pass their caution to naive ones. They re-ran the analysis with 500 km and 2,000 km regions and with the whalers' own named grounds, and the picture held. Their supplement also notes that days from one voyage are related, so the error bars may be too narrow. That note is where we started.

01Re-running it from their own files

The supplement holds the data, the MATLAB code and the region rules, under a CC BY 4.0 licence. We rebuilt the rules in Python on the same file, with no new data.

02Shaking it, one voyage at a time

The file's "voyage" field holds voyage segments: 1,091 within-year pieces of 240 voyages. Days from one voyage share a captain, a crew and a run of luck, so we resampled whole voyages, 10,000 times, refitting the curve each time.

03Where the early evidence lives

The decline is carried by the first three years after onset (T = 0 to 2). Those years hold 190 sighting-days from 18 voyages.

Regions first sighted in the data in 1822 hold 138 of the 190 early days and 1,837 of the 2,321 days in the whole fit. Nearly all of those days (1,762) lie on the grounds the authors labelled Japan, Japan-East, Japan-North and Coast of Japan, after the whalers' own names. No other onset year holds more than 11 early days. Four voyages, all of which sailed in 1821, supply 148 of the 190; the other 14 supply 42.

On that ground the story is vivid: in its first three years whalers struck about 0.99 whales per day of sightings, and later 0.41. That is the famous drop at its clearest, and it is one regional event: the first logged years of the Japan grounds, seen mostly through four voyages' logs.

04Take that ground away

The paper's title asks about learning on a large scale. So the natural test is to set the 1822 regions aside and see whether the rest of the North Pacific tells the same story. We wrote the rule down before running it.

The rule, fixed in advance. ROBUST only if the full-data voyage interval excludes zero and, without the 1822 regions, the drop is at least 29% (half the published 58%) and its voyage interval also excludes zero. FRAGILE if either interval includes zero.

What is left is 484 sighting-days from 88 voyages, 52 of them early. The plainest measure is a before-and-after comparison with no curve: whalers struck 0.88 whales per sighting-day in a region's first three years and 0.61 afterwards, a 31% drop (the same comparison on all the data gives 53%). Its 95% voyage interval runs from -37% to 56%, so it includes zero. The paper's fitted curve gives 44.5%, above the 29% bar, but with so few early days it often cannot settle, and its interval runs from -52% to over 1,000%. Both intervals include zero.

Under our rule, that is FRAGILE: outside the 1822 regions, the data are too thin to confirm the drop or to rule it out. The drop is clear where the logs first record whaling in 1822 and points the same way elsewhere, so the large-scale reading rests mostly on one region. Whether the whales learned remains open.

The famous drop is real in these logs. Most of its early evidence was written down by four voyages on one ground.

05Could fresh logbooks settle it?

The clean test of a pattern uses data its authors never saw. The Providence Public Library's Nicholson Whaling Collection has 751 digitised items on the Internet Archive, free to reuse. We matched each one against the American Offshore Whaling Logbook database (AOWL), which the 2021 study drew on: 159 are in it, 502 are not, and 90 are partial or uncertain matches. We sealed all 592 as future test data and opened none of their pages.

We tested an AI reader on four logbooks already in AOWL, from voyages of 1831 to 1841. It agreed with AOWL's abstracts on 86 of 97 days (88.7%) and caught 9 of 10 strike days, but flagged only 13 of the 20 encounter days. Our go-line was 90%, so the reader needs one more pass. (AOWL: New Bedford Whaling Museum, Nantucket Historical Association and Mystic Seaport Museum, CC BY 4.0.)

The harder problem is timing. Projected from the sealed logbooks' dates and destinations, they would add about 61 sighting-days from a region's first three years, 41.9 of them from two voyages that sailed in 1822. The bar we set in advance was 150.

The early logs do exist. The voyage database lists 63 voyages that left for the Pacific (or for no recorded ground) in 1819-1824 and have a surviving logbook outside AOWL. Its repository entries put most of them at the New Bedford Whaling Museum (30 entries, including its Kendall collection) and the Nantucket Historical Association (12); a voyage can have logs in more than one place, so these counts may overlap. We could find only 2 of the 63 on whalinghistory.org's list of scanned logs, the two Nicholson voyages above, and we did not check the museums' own catalogues.

06The part where we try to talk you out of it

Our own weak points, in brief.

  • The verdict turns on the unit. Resample the file's voyage-year segments as if independent, and the interval without 1822 becomes 19.2% to 998%, which excludes zero and would read ROBUST. We fixed whole voyages as the unit in advance, because one crew and one captain carry from year to year. A careful analyst could weigh that differently, and we would like to hear the case.
  • The 1822 cut was chosen with eyes open. Our earlier check is what showed how concentrated the early years are. The rule was written before this analysis, but the cut was informed by the data. A fully blind test needs new logbooks.
  • The onset year is the first logged year. Onsets come from the first sighting in a database that holds about one voyage in ten, so "1822" marks when whaling first shows up in these logs; hunting in a region may have begun earlier. We held onsets at their full-data dates, as the paper's curve does.
  • A pattern is one question, its cause another. Whaler skill, the most vulnerable whales dying first, and changes in what log-keepers chose to record can all bend the same curve. The authors modelled several of these; we tested none.
  • Our reader is short of its own bar. It scored 88.7% against a 90% go-line, and its misses cluster on sighting-only days, the denominator of the strike rate.
  • Novelty, bounded. OpenAlex lists 56 works citing the paper (checked 13 November 2026). None of their titles, and none of the 51 abstracts available (91%), describes a re-analysis of the drop. We have not read their full texts, so someone may already have made this point.

07What it means, and the size of it

The 2021 result stands where the data are dense: the first logged years of the Japan grounds, as recorded by a handful of voyages in 1822 and 1823. There the drop is large, stable under every voyage-level shake we tried, and clearly recorded. The broader reading in the paper's title, learning on a large scale, rests mostly on that one ground. Elsewhere the logs lean the same way and hold too few early days to say more.

That is a data problem with a data answer: read the early logs. Many of the 63 early voyages we could not find scanned have logbooks recorded at New Bedford and Nantucket. With those logs, the sealed Nicholson books and a reader that clears its bar, we could give the question a fair, pre-registered test, ideally one the original authors help design.

To Hal Whitehead, Tim Smith and Luke Rendell: thank you for publishing the files that made this possible. We would value your view on three things: whether the voyage is the right unit for resampling, whether a leave-one-ground-out check changes how you read the large-scale question, and which early logbooks you know of. If we have something wrong, we will correct it here.

An open dataset, a famous curve and a sealed shelf of logbooks. The next step is reading the right ones.

Don't panic. Bring a held-out set.
