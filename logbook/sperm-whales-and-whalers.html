Logbook · entry 04 · a second look at a famous result

One ground, four voyages

In 2021 three researchers reported that American whalers got much worse at striking sperm whales within a few years of reaching each new North Pacific region, and asked whether the whales had learned from one another how to get away. They published every file needed to check it. When we reran it, the result reproduced and held voyage by voyage. But we found most of the early evidence in regions whose first sighting in the 2021 data falls in 1822, mainly the Japan grounds, and in the logs of four voyages. Guide rating: sturdy on one ground, unproven on the rest

Hidden Tide · the logbook, archive edition · 2026

Updated 13 November 2026: clearer labels for what comes from the original papers and what is our own analysis.

The one-paragraph version. Whitehead, Smith and Rendell (2021, Biology Letters) report that the share of sighted sperm whales that whalers struck fell by about 58% within a few years of whaling reaching a North Pacific region, and ask whether rapid social learning explains it. When we reran their published data, the drop reproduced and survived voyage resampling. But most of the early evidence comes from regions first logged in 1822 and from four voyages. Without those regions, our before-and-after comparison gives a 31% drop, with a 95% interval (-37% to 56%) that includes zero. The famous result stands where the data are dense; its "large scale" part needs more early logbooks.

What comes from where.

From the published work

  • The claim: whalers' strike rate on sighted sperm whales fell by about 58%, over a time scale of about 2.4 years, after whaling reached a North Pacific region, possibly through rapid social learning (Whitehead, Smith and Rendell, 2021).
  • The data and code behind it, including the 2,405 sighting-days, published openly with the paper under CC BY 4.0 (Whitehead, Smith and Rendell, 2021, supplement).
  • The daily whaling records it draws on, published as the American Offshore Whaling Logbook database by the New Bedford Whaling Museum, Nantucket Historical Association and Mystic Seaport Museum, CC BY 4.0 (AOWL, 2025).

Our own checks

  • We reproduced their model on their data (58.19% and 2.45 years) and resampled whole voyages (95% interval 41.9% to 69.3%).
  • We measured where the early evidence sits (the 1822-onset regions and four voyages) and reran the drop without those regions (31%, interval -37% to 56%).
  • We matched 751 Providence Public Library logbook items against AOWL, projected how many early sighting-days the 502 outside it could add (about 61, against 150 needed), and tested an AI reader on four logbooks (88.7% agreement with AOWL).

00A famous drop

A nineteenth-century whaling logbook is a diary of encounters: each day, where the ship was, whether whales were seen, whether the boats got one fast. Maury abstracted many such logs for his 1851 whale chart, and the Census of Marine Life digitised more. By the authors' estimate, these records cover roughly one American whaling voyage in ten.

Hal Whitehead, Tim Smith and Luke Rendell used those days to ask a sharp question. For each day sperm whales were sighted in the North Pacific between 10 and 50 degrees north, they looked back for the first sighting within 1,000 km: the moment whaling, as far as the logs know, reached that patch of ocean. Then they counted whales struck per day of sightings, year by year. In their words, the share of sighted whales that whalers "succeeded in harpooning (striking)... fell by about 58% over the first few years", and they report a fitted time scale of about 2.4 years.

Their title ends in a question mark, and the paper earns it. The authors weighed rival explanations: better first whalers, the most vulnerable whales dying first, and each family unit learning from its own close calls. Of the causal models they fitted, the best was one in which wary groups pass their caution to naive ones. They re-ran the analysis with 500 km and 2,000 km regions and with the whalers' own named grounds, and report that the picture held. Their supplement also notes that days from one voyage are related, so the error bars may be too narrow. That note is where we started.

03Where the early evidence lives

The decline is carried by the first three years after onset (T = 0 to 2). In the authors' data those years hold 190 sighting-days from 18 voyages. When we sorted them, they clustered.

No other onset year holds more than 11 early days. On the 1822 ground we count about 0.99 whales struck per day of sightings in the first three years, and 0.41 later. That is the famous drop at its clearest, and it is one regional event: the first logged years of the Japan grounds, seen mostly through four voyages' logs.

04Take that ground away

The paper's title asks about learning on a large scale, so we set the 1822 regions aside to see whether the rest of the North Pacific tells the same story, under a rule written down in advance.

Our rule, fixed in advance. ROBUST only if the full-data voyage interval excludes zero and, without the 1822 regions, the drop is at least 29% (half the published 58%) and its voyage interval also excludes zero. FRAGILE if either interval includes zero.

What is left is 484 sighting-days from 88 voyages, 52 of them early. Our plainest measure, a before-and-after comparison with no curve, gives a 31% drop (0.88 whales struck per sighting-day in the first three years, 0.61 afterwards; 53% on all the data), with a 95% voyage interval of -37% to 56%. Refitting the paper's curve gives 44.5%, above the 29% bar, but with so few early days the curve often cannot settle, and its interval runs from -52% to over 1,000%. Both intervals include zero.

Under our rule, that is FRAGILE: outside the 1822 regions, the data are too thin to confirm the drop or to rule it out, so the large-scale reading rests mostly on one region. Whether the whales learned remains open.

The famous drop is real in these logs. Most of its early evidence was written down by four voyages on one ground.

05Could fresh logbooks settle it?

The clean test of a pattern uses data its authors never saw. The Providence Public Library's Nicholson Whaling Collection has 751 digitised items on the Internet Archive, free to reuse. We matched each one against AOWL, sealed the unmatched and uncertain ones as future test data, and opened none of their pages.

Our reader test used four logbooks already in AOWL, from voyages of 1831 to 1841. It caught 9 of 10 strike days but flagged only 13 of the 20 encounter days, so it needs one more pass. (AOWL: New Bedford Whaling Museum, Nantucket Historical Association and Mystic Seaport Museum, CC BY 4.0.) The harder problem is timing: 41.9 of the projected 61 early days come from just two voyages that sailed in 1822.

The early logs do exist: the voyage database lists 63 voyages that left for the Pacific (or no recorded ground) in 1819-1824 with a surviving logbook outside AOWL, most with entries at the New Bedford Whaling Museum (30, including its Kendall collection) and the Nantucket Historical Association (12), counts that may overlap. We found only 2 of the 63 on whalinghistory.org's list of scanned logs, and we did not check the museums' own catalogues.

06The part where we try to talk you out of it

Our own weak points, in brief.

  • The verdict turns on the unit. Resample the file's voyage-year segments as if independent, and the interval without 1822 becomes 19.2% to 998%, which excludes zero and would read ROBUST. We fixed whole voyages as the unit in advance, because one crew and one captain carry from year to year. A careful analyst could weigh that differently.
  • The 1822 cut was chosen with eyes open. Our earlier check showed how concentrated the early years are. The rule was written before this analysis, but the cut was informed by the data.
  • The onset year is the first logged year. Onsets come from the first sighting in records that cover about one voyage in ten, so "1822" marks when whaling first shows up in these logs; hunting may have begun earlier.
  • A pattern is one question, its cause another. Whaler skill, the most vulnerable whales dying first, and changes in what log-keepers chose to record can all bend the same curve. The authors modelled several of these; we tested none.
  • Our reader is short of its own bar, and its misses cluster on sighting-only days, the denominator of the strike rate.
  • Novelty, bounded. OpenAlex lists 56 works citing the paper (checked 13 November 2026). None of their titles, and none of the 51 abstracts available (91%), describes a re-analysis of the drop. We have not read their full texts, so someone may already have made this point.

07What it means, and the size of it

In our analysis, the 2021 result stands where the data are dense: the first logged years of the Japan grounds, as recorded by a handful of voyages in 1822 and 1823. The broader reading in the paper's title, learning on a large scale, rests mostly on that one ground. Elsewhere the logs lean the same way and hold too few early days to say more.

That is a data problem with a data answer: read the early logs. With the early New Bedford and Nantucket logs, the sealed Nicholson books and a reader that clears its bar, we could give the question a fair, pre-registered test, ideally one the original authors help design.

To Hal Whitehead, Tim Smith and Luke Rendell: thank you for publishing the files that made this possible. We would value your view on three things: whether the voyage is the right unit for resampling, whether a leave-one-ground-out check changes how you read the large-scale question, and which early logbooks you know of. If we have something wrong, we will correct it here.

Don't panic. Bring a held-out set.
