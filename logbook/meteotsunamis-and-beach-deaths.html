Logbook · entry 03 · a dispatch from the surf zone

Meteotsunamis and beach deaths: a first look

Some tsunamis are made by the weather, and on Lake Michigan one set up dangerous rip currents. So we asked the open records a beach-safety question: on US Atlantic and Gulf beaches, do surf-zone deaths and injuries coincide with meteotsunamis more often than chance? On the three years we opened, casualty days showed no excess on the days our detector flagged. The data still cannot rule out a doubling, and the detector mostly misses the smallest waves. Here is the check, its limits, and what a full test would need. Guide rating: an honest null, door left open

Hidden Tide · the logbook, event edition · 2026

The one-paragraph version. We ran a meteotsunami detector on 29 NOAA tide gauges from Massachusetts to Texas for 2013, 2016 and 2017, and lined it up against the surf-zone casualty entries in NOAA's Storm Events database for the same years. Of 107 casualty entries near a working gauge, 9 fell on days the detector flagged, where their own comparison days predicted about 7.9. That is an odds ratio of 1.20, with a 95% interval from 0.43 to 2.71: no excess, and no ruling out a doubling either. The detector flags far more days than NOAA counts meteotsunamis, so read "flagged day" as a day with a meteotsunami-like oscillation at a nearby gauge. Our own rule allowed a full test on the 2019 to 2025 records; we stopped at the first look, and those years stay sealed and untouched for whoever runs it next.

00The wave that rides in on the weather

A meteotsunami is a tsunami-like wave pushed by the atmosphere. A squall line or pressure jump moves over shallow water, the sea piles up beneath it, and harbours and shelves can ring for hours afterwards. They are common. NOAA's own climatology, by Gregory Dusek and colleagues in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society (2019), counted 548 of them at 125 tide gauges on the US East Coast between 1996 and 2017, with hot spots around Cape Hatteras, Wrightsville Beach, Myrtle Beach, Duck and Atlantic City.

And a small one can still be dangerous. In 2019 Álvaro Linares and colleagues published "Unexpected rip currents induced by a meteotsunami" in Scientific Reports, showing that a meteotsunami less than 10 centimetres high drove dangerous rip currents on Lake Michigan. It is the kind of paper that changes how you read a beach: the hazard can arrive with a wave you would barely notice. The University of Wisconsin-Madison's release on that work noted that National Weather Service beach hazard statements "include conditions for rip currents but not for meteotsunamis". (That was in 2019; NOAA's 2021 review describes forecast offices issuing statements when a meteotsunami is detected.)

That leaves a question open data can start to answer. If meteotsunamis can make rip currents, do they show up in the casualty records of US ocean beaches? In the sources we searched, we found no earlier systematic test of that on the Atlantic and Gulf coasts. That search was not exhaustive.

One documented case shows the mechanism. The beach records can tell us something about the rate.

01How we asked it

Three pieces of free, public data, and one design.

The water

NOAA's tide gauges record sea level every six minutes. Our detector, built and frozen in an earlier check, strips out the tide and flags any burst of 18-to-120-minute oscillation at least 10 cm from crest to trough and at least six times livelier than that gauge's recent background. On waves we planted in the real records, it caught 95% of those 10 to 20 cm high and all of those 20 to 40 cm. On the real data, 17% of its events had a sharp air-pressure jump in the hour before onset, against 1% of comparison windows, which ties a real share of them to the weather. Others may be harbour sloshing of other kinds, and that matters later.

The detector is also far more permissive than NOAA's climatology. At Atlantic City it logged 139 events in three years, about 46 a year, where the climatology counts more than five a year. Its stricter setting (20 cm and ten times the background) logged 23 there, about 8 a year, closer to NOAA's scale. So in this post a flagged day means a day the detector caught a meteotsunami-like oscillation at a gauge near the beach, and many flagged days would never make NOAA's list.

The casualty records

NOAA's Storm Events database logs deaths and injuries by event type. We took every Atlantic and Gulf entry typed rip current, high surf, sneaker wave, tsunami or seiche with at least one death or injury: 158 entries across the three years, 149 of them rip currents. We count entries and describe no individual case.

The comparison

For each casualty day we asked one thing: did any gauge within 50 km log a detector event that calendar day? Then we asked the same of its comparison days, the same weekday one, two, three and four weeks earlier or later, inside the same month. Epidemiologists call this a case-crossover design. Each casualty serves as its own control (same stretch of coast, same month, same weekday), so season, weekend crowds and local habits cancel out.

Why only 2013, 2016 and 2017? The 2019 to 2025 casualty and gauge records are sealed, so that a real test can run on data we have never looked at, with 2018 as a buffer. Before measuring the extra gauges, we also wrote down what would stop us: if the top of the 95% interval came in below 2, we would close the question as a bounded null.

02The first look, then a wider net

The detector already ran on 12 gauges. Near them, 40 casualty entries qualified, 2 fell on flagged days, and their comparison days predicted 3.4. Odds ratio 0.56, interval 0 to 2.12. Too few cases to say much, and most beach casualties sat near 18 other gauges we had never measured. So we measured 17 of them on the same open years (the 18th, a river station, was excluded by design), with the detector unchanged.

03Twenty-nine gauges, one answer

With 29 gauges, 107 casualty entries had a working gauge and at least one usable comparison day. Nine fell on flagged days. Their own comparison days predicted about 7.9.

An odds ratio of 1 would mean casualty days and their comparison days were equally likely to be flagged days; 2 would mean twice the odds. We got 1.20, a nudge that chance produces easily. All 9 exposed entries were rip currents between May and August.

Then we cut it every way we had written down in advance: stricter location and detector rules, a 100 km radius, hours instead of days, deaths only, no bay or inlet gauges, each coast on its own, and an adjustment for offshore wave height. Every interval spans 1. Every upper end sits above 2. On the stricter setting, closer to NOAA's scale, just 1 of the 107 casualty entries fell on a flagged day (odds ratio 0.83, interval 0 to 7.5).

04The gauges are not alike

In the beach season, the typical gauge fires on about 4% of days, and the 16 new gauges with data matched the first 12 closely. Leaving aside two suspect gauges, the busiest are Atlantic City, Duck, Wrightsville Beach and Hatteras, the hot spots the NOAA climatology had already mapped, which is consistent with that climatology. Six gauges in Florida and the Gulf fire on 0.6% to 2.4% of beach-season days, so 22 of the 26 cases that carry information (a flagged casualty day or a flagged comparison day) come from the Atlantic coast north of Florida.

One gauge stands out. Fort Pulaski, at a Georgia river entrance with a median daily tidal range of 2.3 m, fires on 31% of all days and supplies 3 of the 9 exposed cases. A check of tidal timing did not explain it. Drop it and the estimate is 0.98 (0.27 to 2.39); drop it with Trident Pier, the other outlier, and it is 1.08 (0.28 to 2.62). Both checks came after the result (post hoc), and both move the number toward 1.

05The part where we try to talk you out of it

A soft null deserves the same scrutiny as a hit. Here is what could hide a real effect, or fake one.

  • Rough seas run both ways. Rough seas trip the detector: on the roughest quarter of days it fired 12.7% of the time, against 3.8% on other days. Rough seas also make rip currents: casualty days had offshore waves 0.21 m higher than their comparison days (0.06 to 0.36), and each extra metre came with 1.45 times the odds of a casualty. On the 90 casualty entries with buoy data, the estimate was 1.48 before adjusting for waves and 1.41 (0.43 to 3.66) after. Any future positive has to survive that adjustment, fixed in advance.
  • Places are approximate. Storm Data puts a casualty in a forecast zone, so each case sits at a zone centroid, often tens of kilometres from the sand. Many coastal zones were redrawn after 2017, and the rule that placed those cases was added after a first run. Under the stricter rule as first written, 61 cases stay in and the estimate is 0.95 (0.11 to 3.57).
  • Times are coarse. We matched by calendar day because Storm Data clock times are approximate. Matched by hours instead, 1 of the 100 cases with a usable time was exposed. A meteotsunami rings for hours, while a day-level match counts a whole day, which can dilute a true link.
  • Small waves slip through. The Lake Michigan meteotsunami in Linares and colleagues' paper was under 10 cm. Our detector needs about 10 cm, and on planted waves of 5 to 10 cm it caught only 12%. It would mostly miss a wave like that one, so this check speaks only to the larger oscillations at ocean gauges.
  • The count may be incomplete. The NWS keeps its own rip-current fatality lists. We did not compare them, because their only index page sits inside the sealed years.
  • The sample is small. 107 cases can only rule out big effects. This one makes odds ratios above about 2.7 unlikely and leaves everything below open.
  • None of this contradicts Linares and colleagues. Their paper establishes a mechanism; ours asks about a rate, on different coasts, with coarser data and a detector tuned to larger oscillations.

06Why we stopped, and what a full test would need

Our stop rule pointed the other way. Because the top of the interval (2.71) sat above 2, it called for designing a full test on the sealed 2019 to 2025 records: about 218 eligible cases and a 96% chance of catching a tripling of the odds. We stopped at the first look anyway, and logged it as a departure from our own rule. A full run would have had a 44% chance of catching a doubling and, if there is no link at all, would most likely have narrowed the interval to an upper end near 1.8. That is worth having for beach safety, and the sealed years are kept for it.

Whoever picks this up would need:

  • The sealed records, opened once. We have never opened the 2019 to 2025 casualty and gauge data; they open after the design is frozen.
  • Wave height as a fixed adjustment, with each gauge's buoy named in advance and a stand-in for the one buoy that returned no files.
  • A drop-each-gauge check and a per-gauge rate check, so a single odd gauge like Fort Pulaski cannot carry the answer.
  • Locations fixed before any gauge is read: a zone map for each year, or casualty narratives geocoded and frozen first.
  • A cleaner exposure as a secondary: only detector events with a confirmed air-pressure jump.
  • A completeness check of Storm Data against the NWS rip-current fatality lists.

If you know this territory better, we would like your read. Linares and colleagues: would a calendar-day match wash out the effect you saw? NOAA's meteotsunami team: is there an event list after 2017 that could stand in for our detector? The NWS Storm Data program: how should a rip current driven by a meteotsunami be logged? Tell us where we went wrong, and we would add your answer here.

07What it means

A null is a result. On the open years, beach casualty days near tide gauges were not detectably more likely than their own comparison days to be days our detector flagged. For anyone who writes beach forecasts, the question stays open with a ceiling: the data fit both no link and an effect up to about 2.7 times the odds, and they speak only to the larger oscillations our detector can see. The sealed years stay untouched for the test that can narrow it.

No excess on the open years. A doubling still fits. The sealed years are waiting.

Don't panic. Bring a held-out set.
