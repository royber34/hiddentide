Logbook · entry 05 · a check on a famous number

One-third of what?

In 2011 a team of seabird and fisheries scientists gave ocean management a rule you can say in one breath: leave one-third for the birds. We rebuilt their analysis from the paper's own supplement. The threshold lands where they said it does, near the long-term average of the prey. The 34.6% behind "one-third of the maximum" is a position on a pooled ruler of standardized values. In a stock's own units the share differs: for 10 of the 19 seabird-prey pairs a firm lower bound sits above one-third, and in the one stock we could measure directly (Norwegian herring, counted as young fish) it is 16%. Guide rating: mind the ruler

By Roy Bernheim, working with AI agents. The approach, the judgment, and any mistakes are his.

Hidden Tide · the logbook · November 2026

Updated 13 November 2026: clearer labels for what comes from the original papers and what is our own analysis. We first wrote that we could not open Guillemette et al. 2018; it is open access, and this version credits its gannet results.

The one-paragraph version. Cury and colleagues report that seabird breeding success starts to fall when prey drops to about its long-term average, which they expressed as 34.6% of the maximum observed prey abundance. Their script computes that number as the threshold's position on a pooled range of standardized values, which equals a share of a stock's maximum only if the stock's worst year was near zero. In a stock's own units the share differs: for 10 of the 19 pairs a firm lower bound sits above one-third, Norwegian herring (counted as young fish) sits at 16.2%, and Guillemette and colleagues report 12.4% for gannets with Cury's method. Seabirds need food; the question is which ruler the number was measured on.

What comes from where.

From the published work:

  • Breeding success starts to fall near the prey's long-term mean, reported as 34.6% (95% confidence interval 31% to 39%) of the maximum observed prey abundance: "one-third for the birds" (Cury et al. 2011).
  • The authors' per-series thresholds run from 22% to 61%, with a 28% coefficient of variation (Cury et al. 2011, Table S1).
  • For northern gannets and Atlantic mackerel, in tonnes: a threshold at 12.4% of the maximum with Cury's method, about 8% with a segmented regression (Guillemette et al. 2018).
  • Standardising on a single extreme value is not statistically robust (Butterworth 2015; Guillemette and colleagues make the same point).

Our own checks:

  • We rebuilt the pipeline from the published scripts and data and got 34.67% (reported by Cury et al., reproduced by us).
  • We read the conversion line: the 34.6% is the threshold's position on the pooled range of z-scores.
  • We measured the share of maximum for the one stock we could match to raw data (Norwegian herring, 16.2%) and bounded the rest: for 10 of the 19 pairs the lower bound sits above one-third.
  • We ran the method on 2,000 simulated datasets with no threshold: medians of 35.7% and 37.7%, and a pre-set verdict of inconclusive.

00A rule you can say in one breath

The paper is Cury et al. 2011, "Global seabird response to forage fish depletion – one-third for the birds", in Science. It is an ambitious, carefully documented piece of work. The team gathered 19 series that pair a seabird's breeding success with an index of its main prey (fish and krill, which they call "forage fish"): 14 bird species, seven ecosystems, 438 paired observations. Each series was standardized to its own mean and spread, so all 19 could share one chart.

They report a plateau in breeding success when prey is plentiful, and a fall, with more variable breeding, when it is scarce. In their words:

"The threshold at which breeding success began to decline from the asymptote was not significantly different from the long-term mean of prey abundance (range -0.30 and +0.13 standard deviation of the mean, Fig. 2A). The threshold was 34.6% (95% confidence interval 31% to 39%), or approximately one-third of the maximum observed prey abundance."

From that they proposed a guideline for managers: to "maintain forage fish biomass above one-third of the maximum observed long-term biomass". A rule that short travels: by 2015, South Africa's pelagic scientific working group was weighing it as a fishery performance threshold.

The authors did something that makes a check like this possible at all: their supplement prints every standardized data point and the R scripts behind the threshold. So we rebuilt the pipeline and ran it on their data.

02The line that makes "one-third"

The last step is one line of R, exactly as printed in the supplement:

percentage_threshold <- (threshold+abs(min(prey)))/(max(prey)+abs(min(prey)))

The label printed on the plot next to the result reads "% of max prey abundance".

prey is the pooled column of standardized values. Its minimum is the scarcest standardized year across all 19 series: a poor year for rockfish off California (2005, at -1.91 standard deviations from that series' mean). Its maximum is the richest: a boom year for young herring off Norway (2002, at +3.34). The formula measures how far along that ruler the threshold (-0.092) sits: 34.7% of the way.

So the 34.6% is a position. Because the bend is close to the mean (zero on this scale), it mostly reports where the mean falls on that ruler: 36.4% for these data.

The guideline reads the same number as a share: keep each stock above one-third of its own maximum biomass. That is a different quantity, and the two meet only in a special case.

03From the ruler to the water

For a single stock, call p the threshold's position between that stock's lowest and highest years, and r its lowest year divided by its highest. Then the threshold's share of the maximum is r + (1 - r) × p. If the stock once fell to almost nothing, r is near zero and share equals position. If its worst year still held plenty of fish, the share is larger.

The authors saw stock-to-stock spread themselves (Table S1: 22% to 61%), though their values come from each series' own curve, by a conversion the supplement does not show, so they may not compare directly with ours; if it used raw units, part of this per-stock point was in their paper from the start.

Standardizing hides how variable each stock was and how low it went, so this step needs raw series. Before reading any, we fixed a rule: a raw series counts only if its standardized values track the paper's at a correlation of 0.98 or better, year for year.

One stock passed. Norwegian spring-spawning herring, counted as age-0 recruits in the RAM Legacy stock-assessment database, matches the paper's prey series with a correlation of 0.992 over 45 years. This stock went through a long collapse from the 1960s to the 1980s, and its worst year held 0.06% of the young fish of its best. So r is close to zero, and the threshold's share of the maximum is close to its position: 16.2%.

Guillemette and colleagues got there first for another stock. In 2018 they ran Cury's method on northern gannets and Atlantic mackerel in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, in tonnes, and report the threshold at 12.4% of the maximum mackerel biomass (about 8% with their own segmented regression). Our herring 16.2% sits beside their gannet 12.4%. What our check adds is why such shares part company with 34.6%: that number is a position on the pooled z-scale, and a stock's share of its own maximum depends on how low the stock went.

For the other 17 pairs no raw series passed, so we bound them. Each pair has a floor, the share it would have if its worst year were zero. If the stocks were about as variable as the published data suggest (a coefficient of variation, the spread relative to the average, of 0.3 to 0.6), the threshold would land at roughly 40% to 60% of each stock's maximum. That band is at the low end for small pelagic fish, and the two raw series we saw swung more (0.8 and 1.5), so only the floor is a firm bound.

The generous reading: as a summary of where the threshold sits within each stock's own range, the pooled 34.6% is a fair middle value (the per-pair positions have a median of 34.3%, or 32.7% counting each prey series once). The step that needs care is the one from "a third of the way up the range" to "a third of the maximum".

04A second check: worlds with no threshold

We built 1,000 synthetic datasets for each of two threshold-free worlds, matched to the paper's 19 series in length, gaps and prey shape, and ran the pipeline on all 2,000. In one world, breeding success rises in a straight line with prey; in the other, it follows a smooth curve that levels off with no sharp bend, its half-way point placed at random for each series. That curve has the broad shape the paper describes, which is the point: the test asks whether the method finds "one-third" in a curve with no single bend to find.

Our rule, written before any run: at least half the runs inside 31-39% in either world would mean an artefact of the method; under a fifth in both, a clean result. Neither happened, so the verdict is inconclusive. The method lands near one-third in these worlds often enough that the number's location, taken alone, is weak evidence for where a biological threshold sits, and too unevenly to prove an artefact. This test speaks only to where the number lands; the authors' other evidence for a threshold, such as the rise in variance below it, is a separate matter we did not test.

Part of the pull comes from the search window: the script looks for the bend only in the lowest 150 of its 200 grid points, and lifting that limit moves the straight-line world's median from 35.7% to 49.0%. The real data behave differently: lifting it there barely moves the published threshold (34.7% to 34.2%), which is mild evidence of a bend in their data beyond the window's pull.

05The part where we try to talk you out of it

Before anyone else makes it, here is the case against us.

  • The authors said it first. Their text places the threshold at the long-term mean of prey, and that result reproduces exactly. They also report a robustness check, a mixed model allowing for differences between ecosystems, that put it at -0.12, close to the main -0.092. Our point concerns the conversion to "one-third of the maximum" and the guideline built on it.
  • One measured stock. Two pairs, one series, one long collapse: the kind of stock where the share sits lowest. One near-miss points the other way: the African penguin pair matches South Africa's sardine-plus-anchovy survey biomass with a correlation of 0.991, but only with a one-year shift our rule did not allow, and there the threshold sits at 34.4% of the maximum.
  • The threshold-free test leans on assumptions. With more variable prey (a coefficient of variation of 0.6 to 1.2), the smooth-curve world puts 50.4% of runs inside the window, just over our artefact bar, and the simulated worlds are simple.
  • Has anyone said this before? In part. Guillemette and colleagues (2018) showed a per-stock share far below one-third, and argued that a threshold tied to "a single (and often extreme) value of food abundance" is "not sufficiently robust", an argument Butterworth (2015) had already made about standardising on one extreme value. Both read the 34% as a share of the maximum. Neither of them, nor any citing paper we could search (about 45% of the works citing Cury et al.), describes it as a position on a pooled standardized range. A note could exist in a report we did not reach.
  • Whether seabirds need food. They do, and keeping enough forage fish in the water for them is the purpose of the rule. Nothing here makes a case for lower limits: for 10 of the 19 pairs even the floor sits above one-third, and the 16% is one stock, counted in young fish rather than tonnes.

06What we would ask the authors

We would welcome their view on four questions:

  1. Was "% of max prey abundance" meant as a position on the pooled standardized range, and how would you translate it for one stock?
  2. How were the per-series percentages in Table S1 computed?
  3. Do raw prey series exist for the sandeel, krill and rockfish pairs?
  4. Would a rule stated relative to each stock's own long-term mean carry your result more directly than a share of the maximum?

If we have misread the script, we want to know.

Keep enough fish for the birds. And when the rule travels, send the ruler with it.

Don't panic. Bring a held-out set, and check the units.
